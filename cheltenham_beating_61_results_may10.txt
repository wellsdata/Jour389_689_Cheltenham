Carol-A Dance or Dance Music Carol-To Sing in a cheerful or joyous strain to
warble, torn dance and sing a dance performed in a circle, also a song
accompanying it, a choral dance.- Webster's Dictionary. Yes, if they want to.
It is their business and nobody's else. Persons accustomed to regard the
Christmas season as a serious and stilled celebration must have gotten a shock
from Dr. Hugh Birckhead Sunday when he declared that early Christmas music was
written for dance purposes. The rector of Emanuel P. E. Church went further and
described German carols as written in waltz time, French in march time, and the
Russian carols in polka time. Commenting on the French carols, he added that
there was no emphasis on the child Jesus as the son of God, but rather on the
Bethlehem Babe as an infant, cunning, beauti- ful and unexpressibly dear. The
early church, said Dr. Birckhead, emphasized the dance in connection with
Christmas carols. How much danc- ing there was can be seen from the fact that
it required three church councils to abolish it. V Today most churches regard
the dance as sinful in itself C -a thing to be banished to the stage and the
dance hall. St. Mark's Church, New York, advertised a dance interpreting the
Scriptures recently, and drew down upon its head the ban of the bishops. How
far the church ban goes may be seen also in the fact that no dancing is
permitted in the Y. M. C. A.'s and the community houses. Church members dance,
members of the pastor's family dance at home or publicly in dance halls, but as
for church dancing, the thing simply isn't done any more. Cheltenham Again
Grand Juries come and Grand Juries go, but the mis- management of Cheltenham,
(House of Reformation for Col- ored Boys) like Tennison's book, goes on
forever. Reports of the last three Baltimore City Grand Juries pointed out
grave faults in Cheltenham and urged relief. It has been shown that the place
is unsanitary, that young boys are given less than three months schooling; that
they are hired out like convicts, that they are beaten by guards, and by
superintendent. One Grand Jury, of which Mr. Joseph P. Evans was a member, in a
special report pointed out these things and in addition, urged that
Superintendent Pyles, who is now phy- sically unable to perform his duties, be
supplanted. While the institution is privately managed, it receives aid from
the City and State and Baltimore delinquents are sent there from the Juvenile
Court. Last week witnesses appearing before the Grand Jury testified that the
chief diet was bread and tea-seldom meat except in soup. : These are the facts.
Certainly Baltimore Judges cannot continue to send delinquents to such
institutions as cannot help them to become better citizens. . .

a third who has charge of the din- ing room. Punishment of the boys who fail to
keep their eyes lowered is set at fifty lashes on their bare backs. Supt. Pyles

practiced any- where, and persons coming up the steps behind a woman are not

but he said that the rule was necessary at Chelten- ham. He also stated that
the visiting hours at the institution have been limited because of frequent es-
capes. Mr. Pyles stated that four boys escaped last Sunday and have not been
apprehended. Autoist Gives Ald One boy, according to Mr. Pyles, was picked up

rious," he returned and started to change the subject. "Can you describe those

close-quar- ter fight." he stated slowly. He failed to find a way to de- scribe

given by the relatives is: "Bronson was shot by a guard who was yelling at a

had attempted to strike Bronson with a club, but the youth succeeded in
wresting the cudgel from his assailant. "Another guard seized the man in an
attempt to stop him from hurting Bronson, but the latter drew a gun and shot

given of the shooting, although Bronson was sent to the hospital and it was
known that he was suffering from a punctured lung." Office Hadn't Heard The
Baltimore office of the House of Reformation, queried by the AFRO on Tuesday

has condemned the place as unfit and unprepared to give correctional training

has also been scored for sending colored boys to a pri- vate school, conducted

carry them back to the school. So many boys are escaping that Sunday one

boys are barefooted so that they will make no noise at meals.

in- mate, James Tynes. The boys quarrelled, it was said, and Tynes used the bat
in the fight that resulted. Tynes is reported to have been committed to the

running over and fatally wound- ing a girl. Killed His Sweetheart McClellan was
committed to Cheltenham in May after he had been convicted of killing

at the trial was to the effect that he and Miss Cherry quarrelled over his
atten- tions to a Miss Dorothy Wilson, a friend of the slain girl. Miss Cherry

gun and fired. The killing at the time caused considerable interest because of
the sordidness of life it revealed. Orphaned since 1929, the Cherry girl had
lived a precarious life with relatives, it was said. Testi- mony by police
brought out that before she was 15 years of age she was visiting the home of

STILL IN NEED OF REFORM The transfer of Cheltenham to the State was undertaken
largely with the idea that it would put an end to major abuses. The school had
been a target of criticism for years. It was thought that if the State took the
institution over it would be possible quickly to transform it into a reasonably
effective agency for dealing with young Negroes gone wrong. Now, nearly a year
and a half from the time the institution came under the ægis of the State, it
is still necessary for the Employ- ment Commissioner to bring forward charges
growing out of the alleged beating of a 14- year-old boy accused of stealing a
few cigars. If the commissioner's charges are even par- tially true, the
conclusion is inescapable that the benefits which were expected to flow from
the State's assumption of responsibility have not materialized. If such a thing
as he de- scribes can still happen at Cheltenham, the institution is still far
from being the sort of place to which the State can send delinquent Negro boys
in the hope of making them over and turning them out as decent citizens. The
trouble seems to lie in the fact that, when the State took the school over, it
was persuaded also to take its employés into the merit system without
examination. When the Employment Commissioner held exami- nations for purposes
of classification, he found that large numbers of those on the pay rolls at
Cheltenham could not come up even to the minimum requirements of the merit sys-
tem. He refused to classify them, or to ap- prove pay rolls on which these
employés were listed. The Cheltenham board, however, which had also been taken
over by the State, backed up the employés, and the case is still in the courts.
Whatever the legal merits of the commis- sioner's contentions, his charges in
the present case reinforce the point that Cheltenham is in perative need of a
good house cleaning . .

"NOTHING UNUSUAL" The more we hear about the state of affair in Cheltenham the
more in need of thorougl investigation the conduct of that institution seems to
be. The news was bad enough on Saturday, when the State Employment Com
missioner, Mr. Jones, reported to the Gov ernor that a cottage master had hit
one of the inmates on the head with a padlock and that the boy, on emerging
from several days' con finement for the theft of a few cigars, had beer further
beaten with a rubber hose. Now the admission of Mr. Sunderland, the head of the
school, that beating the boys with rubber hose is nothing unusual makes the
picture all the worse. This admission needs to be considered in connection with
the statement of the Employ- ment Commissioner that the conduct card of the
boy, whose punishment is in question in the charges against the cottage master,
showed no record of confinement in a cell, nor a beating. If it is usual to
flog boys at Chelten- ham with a rubber hose, as Mr. Sunderland says, and if
careful records of such floggings are not kept, then it would seem that disci-
pline is on a very helter-skelter basis. And it is decidedly against the public
interest to have discipline on a helter-skelter basis in an insti- tution
dedicated to the reclamation of young Negro delinquents. The work of reforming
colored boys, and making them into good citi- zens who will not be menaces to
society when they are discharged, is too serious a task to be handled except in
the most careful and discriminate manner. The admission as to the usualness of
rubber-hose beatings raises issues entirely too broad to be settled at a
hearing on charges against a single cottage master. The State's responsibility
in this matter requires that the Governor take a hand and arrange for a thor-
ough ventilation of what goes on at Chelten- ham. If the courts should decide
that as the law now stands there can be no house-clean- ing at this
institution, it will be imperative for the next Legislature to change the law,
and it is in order for the Governor now to take appropriate steps to prepare
the way for such a contingency. . .

LABOR LEADERS ATTACK SYSTEM AT CHELTENHAM Charge Exploitation Of Negro Boys At
Private Reform School. SAY PEONAGE PLAN NOW EXISTS THERE Protest Against
Contracts Will Be Filed With Gov- ernor Ritchie. Organized labor in Baltimore
soon will file with Governor Ritchie charges of alleged exploitation, which
they term a form of peonage, of the inmates of the Cheltenham School for Boys,
in Prince George's county, according to Henry F. Broening, president of the
Baltimore Federation of Labor. The Cheltenham school is a negro reformatory and
is supported partly by the State. Broommakers Complain. According to Mr.
Broening, outside contractors are allowed to make use of the boys in the
manufacture of brooms and similar work, to the detriment of the trade in
general. This. in turn, he said, has a depressing effect upon wages paid to
skilled workers in the industry, members of which now are being organ- ized
into a local, affiliated with the Federation of Labor. Mr. Broening,
accompanied by Sam- uel Gompers, president of the American Federation of Labor;
H. L. Eichel- berger, Maryland representative of the central body; Charles L.
Wiegand, sec- retary of the Baltimore Federation of Labor, and several other
labor leaders, called on Governor Ritchie at Annapolis Tuesday and explained
their grievances. Governor Ritchie promised to investi- gate the complaints and
asked Mr. Broening to file with him a brief of the charges. Given Small Pay.
"Boys at the institution are paid from 16 to 18 cents a day for their work in
broommaking," said Mr. Broen- ing, "with the result that contractors employing
them can underbid outside concerns in the same line of business. Even the
Maryland Workshop for the Blind is not able to compete with the cut rates of
the product from the Chel- tenham institution. This sort of edu- cation is not
doing the negroes any good either, because, from investigations we have made,
not one in 100 who come from there follow the trade of broom- making when they
are discharged. "Aside from this, however, is the ele- ment of unfairness all
along the line, as we have been able to observe it. Some of the chain stores
and cut-rate establishments in Baltimore and else- where are enabled, through
their deal- ings with Cheltenham contractors, to offer the product of the shops
at prices which the ordinary dealer, who buys in the open market, cannot
possibly meet. Called Form Of Peonage. "We feel that no State-aided insti-
tution should be used for exploitation in such manner. We understand that the
main object of the Cheltenham school is to educate the boys to some useful
occupation they will follow after leaving the school. Cheltenham, ap- parently,
is not doing this but is being utilized to beat down legitimate labor values to
the enrichment of a few indi- viduals or firms. This we regard as a form of
peonage and exploitation' in its worst form." John B. Pyles, superintendent of
Cheltenham, said that the institution receives 50 cents a day from the con-
tractors. The boys, he said, make from $3 to $7 a week, depending on their
agility and also on their ability to do overtime work. . .
